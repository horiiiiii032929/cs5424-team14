\needspace{4\baselineskip}\section{Task 2: Optimized design for workload A}
The objective is average transaction throughput, with latency percentiles, retries and failures retained to assess the trade-offs. The main progression is a0 to a102 (lookup indexes), a111 (join-key colocation), and a112 (read-only declarations). a113, a114 and a115 test alternative follow-ups. Short diagnostics and cross-allocation comparisons are distinguished from the matched full a0/a112 matrix; the final combined improvement does not isolate each component's effect.\par
\needspace{4\baselineskip}\subsection*{\texttt{expt\_\allowbreak{}a102}: lookup indexes}
Kelvin's a102 job 965657 is independently verified from the raw files: all 12 source files match commit 82058e6 and the saved runtime hashes. It used xcnf1 coordinator/xcnf2-5 workers, PostgreSQL 18.6/Citus 14.1.0, scale 1 and the original benchmark settings. At 1/4/8/16/32/64 clients, TPS was 3.250/3.453/3.543/3.320/3.782/3.398, versus baseline 3.220/2.283/2.285/2.368/2.405/2.283.\par
All six measured a102 exits and measured serialization-failure counts are zero, with six passing core checks. Its 16-client warmup exited 2 and that case is excluded from clean comparisons. Clean concurrent cases show 49-57\% gains over preserved a0 918524, but only approximately -3\% to +6\% against fresh a0 977740. Different node allocations and single observations prevent a matched index-only speedup claim. Source/raw evidence are in results/cluster-import-20261019/. Figure 3.1 retains the historical preserved-baseline comparison and flags the warmup abort.\par

\needspace{4\baselineskip}\subsection*{\texttt{expt\_\allowbreak{}a101}: merchant colocation and failed diagnostics}
a101 distributes merchant-owned data by merchant\_id to make merchant-scoped operations local. Its small-fixture four-client cases aborted. These negative results motivated join-key rather than wholesale merchant colocation. \needspace{4\baselineskip}\subsection*{\texttt{expt\_\allowbreak{}a111}: join-key colocation}
a111 adapts the B team's placement ideas on top of a102: inventory is colocated with products by product\_id, and order\_items with orders by order\_id; T7 uses the corresponding routing predicates. This targets the joins used by T3/T4 while retaining query semantics, SERIALIZABLE isolation, workload weights, durability and retry limits. a112 adds READ ONLY declarations to T3/T4/T6 relative to a111.\par
\needspace{4\baselineskip}\subsection*{\texttt{expt\_\allowbreak{}a113}: safe-snapshot control against a111 and a112}
a113 adds READ ONLY DEFERRABLE to a112's read transactions. The scale-1/four-client diagnostics used the original workload mix, 60-second warmups and 60-second measurements. All three completed both phases without measured failures or retries and passed exact validation.\par

\needspace{4\baselineskip}\subsection*{Supporting implementations and validation scope}
Local-only variants a103-a108 and a109/a110 explore one-call T1, maintained summaries, memory/executor settings and read-only declarations. Their source is retained under \texttt{supporting\_\allowbreak{}implementations/local\_\allowbreak{}only/} and their small-scale outputs under \texttt{evidence/local-only/}; they are not course-cluster benchmark experiments. The original distributed correctness suite passed 138 checks. Follow-up a109/a110 passed 26/38 checks and a111/a112/a113 passed 29 each (151 in total). The local hot-merchant fixture differs from the full cluster dataset, so its timings are not ranked as cluster evidence.\par
\needspace{4\baselineskip}\subsection*{Write-path experimental method}
Workload A is 75\% writes by weight (T1 36\%, T2 20\%, T5 10\%, T7 5\%, T8 2\%, T9 2\%). In parallel with the placement work above, we tested whether the update path itself could be made cheaper: fewer round trips per update, HOT updates, less waiting for WAL flushes, and a fixed lock order for T7. Each experiment starts from \texttt{expt\_\allowbreak{}a102} (job 965657), i.e. the baseline plus a102's three lookup indexes and its post-run correctness check (\texttt{sql/verify.sql}), and tests the changes described below. In particular, a11 uses a14 as its VACUUM/CHECKPOINT control. Workload mix, scale 1, SERIALIZABLE isolation, 60-second warmups, 600-second measurements and full log sampling are unchanged.\par
All runs were pinned to xcnf nodes. A first a12 submission that landed on xcnc nodes (job 967193) was cancelled and resubmitted (967217), since different hardware would confound a disk-sensitive setting. All four completed Slurm jobs exited 0 and passed all six post-run correctness checks; a10/c32 nevertheless has pgbench exit 2 and is excluded from clean comparisons. The invariant checks found no negative inventory, no duplicate order or order-item IDs, and every order total equal to the sum of its items.\par
To observe the update path directly, run.sh also snapshots \texttt{n\_\allowbreak{}tup\_\allowbreak{}upd}, \texttt{n\_\allowbreak{}tup\_\allowbreak{}hot\_\allowbreak{}upd} and \texttt{n\_\allowbreak{}tup\_\allowbreak{}newpage\_\allowbreak{}upd} from \texttt{pg\_\allowbreak{}stat\_\allowbreak{}user\_\allowbreak{}tables} on every worker, for the shards of customers, products, inventory and orders, before and after each measured run, and writes the difference to \texttt{hot\_\allowbreak{}updates.csv}. The snapshots run outside the 600-second window. In a10 this returned no rows, because Citus hides shard tables from catalog queries on workers; from a14 onwards the query first sets \texttt{citus.show\_\allowbreak{}shards\_\allowbreak{}for\_\allowbreak{}app\_\allowbreak{}name\_\allowbreak{}prefixes = '*'}.\par
On this base, overall TPS is 3.0-3.8 for every variant. T3 and T4 still account for about 94\% of recorded time and the four single-row updates about 0.2\%, so the unchanged reads dominate overall throughput. That noise is visible in transactions none of these experiments touch: in the a10 run, T3, T4 and T6 were 9\%, 14\% and 9\% slower at one client than in a102 (T3 2,911.7 vs 2,668.5 ms), and T4 was 53\% slower at 16 clients. We therefore judge each change on the transactions it targets, and regard small throughput differences between these single runs as inconclusive; repeated matched runs are needed to estimate variability.\par
\needspace{4\baselineskip}\subsection*{\texttt{expt\_\allowbreak{}a10}}
T2, T5, T8 and T9 make up 34\% of workload A. In the baseline each is already a single \texttt{UPDATE} (T2's stock check is already part of its \texttt{WHERE} clause), but each is wrapped in \texttt{BEGIN}/\texttt{COMMIT}. That costs three client-to-coordinator round trips instead of one, and adds a separate \texttt{COMMIT} at which Citus can turn a worker's serialization cancellation into the non-retryable shard-commit error shown in Figure 5.4.1.\par
\needspace{4\baselineskip}\subsubsection*{Key Changes}
\begin{itemize}
\item Remove \texttt{BEGIN} and \texttt{COMMIT} from T2, T5, T8 and T9, so each \texttt{UPDATE} runs as one autocommit statement (Figure 3.10.1).
\item The \texttt{UPDATE} text, its inputs and the SERIALIZABLE isolation level are unchanged.
\end{itemize}

\needspace{4\baselineskip}\subsection*{\texttt{expt\_\allowbreak{}a14}}
The load inserts every row and then updates \texttt{orders.total\_\allowbreak{}amount}, leaving dead tuples behind, and its dirty pages are still being flushed by checkpoints when measurement starts. a14 cleans both up before benchmarking. It also serves as the control for a11, which adds fillfactor on top of it.\par
\needspace{4\baselineskip}\subsubsection*{Key Changes}
\begin{itemize}
\item At the end of every data load (before each client count), run \texttt{VACUUM (ANALYZE)} on each of the six tables, one command per table so that Citus propagates each one to the shards.
\item Then run \texttt{CHECKPOINT} on all five nodes.
\end{itemize}

\needspace{4\baselineskip}\subsection*{\texttt{expt\_\allowbreak{}a11}}
When PostgreSQL updates a row it writes a new row version. If the page has free space and no indexed column changes, this is a HOT (heap-only tuple) update: the new version stays on the same page and no index entries are added. Otherwise the new version goes to another page and every index on the table gets a new entry. Tables are loaded with full pages (fillfactor 100), so most inventory updates in a14 moved to a new page. Leaving 20\% free space on each page should make them HOT.\par
\needspace{4\baselineskip}\subsubsection*{Key Changes}
\begin{itemize}
\item Create customers, products, inventory and orders \texttt{WITH (fillfactor = 80)}. a11's schema log confirms \texttt{fillfactor=80} on the shards of all four tables.
\item Keep a14's VACUUM and CHECKPOINT after every load, so a11 differs from a14 only in fillfactor.
\item No index covers an updated column (\texttt{quantity}, \texttt{order\_\allowbreak{}status}, \texttt{email}, \texttt{price}), so every update stays HOT-eligible.
\end{itemize}

\needspace{4\baselineskip}\subsection*{\texttt{expt\_\allowbreak{}a12}}
By default every commit waits until its WAL record is flushed to disk on each node it wrote to. T1 and T7 write to several shards and commit with two-phase commit, so they wait for several flushes. With \texttt{synchronous\_\allowbreak{}commit = off} the commit returns before the flush.\par
\needspace{4\baselineskip}\subsubsection*{Key Changes}
\begin{itemize}
\item Set \texttt{synchronous\_\allowbreak{}commit = off} in \texttt{postgresql.conf} on the coordinator and all four workers (written by \texttt{init\_\allowbreak{}node}).
\item Leave \texttt{shared\_\allowbreak{}buffers} (1 GB), \texttt{max\_\allowbreak{}wal\_\allowbreak{}size} (16 GB) and \texttt{checkpoint\_\allowbreak{}timeout} (30 min) unchanged: no "checkpoints are occurring too frequently" warnings appeared, and each worker's data (estimated under 1 GB) already fits in the buffer pool. The memory a job can use is capped by Slurm's cgroup limit (3,072 MB per CPU \ensuremath{\times} 4 CPUs = 12 GB per node), not by the node's RAM. \texttt{synchronous\_\allowbreak{}commit = local} was not tested because without standbys it behaves the same as \texttt{on}.
\end{itemize}

Single-shard updates look faster at 8 clients, but a14, which does not change commit behaviour, shows similar drops at the same client count (p50 T2 4.76, T5 3.85, T9 3.66 ms), so these cannot be attributed to a12. T1, the transaction that waits for the most flushes, did not improve (one-client p50 11.68 \ensuremath{\rightarrow} 11.86 ms), and overall TPS was -0.4\% to +3.5\% of a102. T1's latency is therefore dominated by its many client round trips rather than by WAL flushes, which supports the one-call T1 approach in a103.\par
The durability cost is real. With \texttt{synchronous\_\allowbreak{}commit = off}, a commit is acknowledged before its WAL reaches disk, so a crash of any node can lose roughly the last 0.6 seconds (three times \texttt{wal\_\allowbreak{}writer\_\allowbreak{}delay}) of transactions that clients were told had committed. The database stays consistent, but those orders and stock changes disappear. Because T1 and T7 commit across shards with two-phase commit, a worker crash could also lose that worker's part of a transaction the coordinator reported as committed. For an order-processing system this is not acceptable for no measurable gain, and we drop a12.\par
\needspace{4\baselineskip}\subsection*{Unrun T7 rewrite proposal (a13)}
a13 planned to rewrite T7 so that it locks its two inventory rows in a fixed order, to prevent deadlocks. The evidence removed its premise. The preserved baseline (918524) recorded zero deadlock failures; all 126 failed transactions at 16/32/64 clients were T7 serialization failures ("could not serialize access due to read/write dependencies"). T7 looked up its rows by \texttt{product\_\allowbreak{}id} while inventory was distributed by \texttt{inventory\_\allowbreak{}id}, so each lookup scanned every inventory shard, and under SERIALIZABLE those scans conflicted with concurrent T1/T2 writes. a102's \texttt{inventory(product\_\allowbreak{}id)} index removed those scans: T7's failures fell to zero and its one-client latency fell from 51.1 to 13.7 ms. a112 goes further by distributing inventory by \texttt{product\_\allowbreak{}id} (similar to expt\_b1), so a lock-order rewrite had nothing left to fix.\par
\needspace{4\baselineskip}\subsection*{Write-path summary}
On the a102 base, write-path tuning cannot move overall throughput while distributed reads take about 94\% of recorded time. a10's autocommit is the one change with a clear, consistent benefit for its own transactions (29-74\% lower median latency at every eligible client count) and is retained as a separately measured result. A combined a10/a112 design has not been benchmarked. a11 shows the HOT mechanism working (100\% HOT updates) but needs a higher update rate to matter. a12 trades durability for no measurable gain, and a14 has no effect. Two lessons carry into the rest of this section: identical code varied by 9-14\% in mean latency between single runs at one client, and more under contention, so smaller differences need repeats on matched nodes; and the client aborts that limit our fastest designs come mainly from T1's commit, not from the single-statement updates.\par
